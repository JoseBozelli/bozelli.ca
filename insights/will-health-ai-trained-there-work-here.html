Will the Health AI Trained There Work Here?

A clinical AI sepsis model, COVID-19, and why an AI model's performance depends on where you use it.

José Carlos Bozelli Jr., PhD · 17 min read · Also on Substack

The alarm that was switched off

In April 2020, a Michigan hospital switched off its clinical AI sepsis-alerting model.¹

The software hadn’t malfunctioned. It was running as designed, using a prediction model deployed in hundreds of American hospitals.²

But the patients had changed.

COVID-19 was bringing a different mix of people into hospitals. Many had fevers and inflammatory symptoms that could resemble signs of sepsis. The relationship between the signals the model relied on and the condition it was supposed to detect had changed. The hospital’s clinical AI oversight committee decided to switch off the alarm system.¹

Using pre-pandemic records, researchers at a Michigan health system found that the widely used clinical AI sepsis-alerting model had missed roughly two out of every three patients who developed sepsis.² That raised another reason to question how much confidence clinicians should place in this tool.

These were two related problems. The pandemic had changed the clinical environment, while the independent evaluation raised questions about how well the model’s reported performance applied at that hospital in the first place.

I find these cases particularly revealing because neither faulty programming nor a defective instrument caused the failures. The model was doing what it had been trained to do. What had changed was the world around it. A model could function exactly as designed and still provide unreliable guidance.

That raises a question that extends well beyond sepsis, or even medicine.

When an AI model performs well in a study, how much of that performance can we expect to carry over into the real world?

Every reported performance score comes from somewhere: a hospital, a group of patients, a particular set of instruments, a period in time.

Those details aren’t secondary. They define the conditions under which the evidence was produced, i.e., they determine what the score means.

The key question is whether that evidence still holds when those conditions change.

A model learns from its surroundings

In the previous article, How Do You Know an AI Learned the Right Thing?, we explored how AI models learn patterns from examples and why a strong performance score doesn’t necessarily tell us what a model has learned.

Another part of that story is worth examining: where those examples came from.

Researchers use the term dataset shift to describe what happens when the data a model encounters in the real world differs from the data used to develop it.¹

This can happen in several ways.

  • Technology changes. A hospital introduces a new scanner, a laboratory changes its assay, or software starts processing measurements differently.

  • The population changes. A model developed at a major academic hospital is introduced into a community clinic, or a system trained largely on one population is used in another.

  • Clinical practice changes. Physicians order tests differently, treatment guidelines evolve, or a new disease changes the patients arriving at the hospital.

Any of these changes can alter the patterns a model relies on.

Imagine a weather forecast developed for an entire city. It may describe the general conditions accurately while missing what happens in a neighborhood near the lake, where temperatures and wind behave differently.

The city forecast isn’t necessarily wrong. It simply doesn’t capture everything that matters in your neighborhood.

AI models face a similar problem, with one key complication: they don't automatically recognize when their training environments change.

A model can continue producing confident predictions long after its original assumptions have become less reliable.

The model’s performance in its original setting may have been entirely genuine. However, a separate scientific question is whether that evidence applies to a new setting.

In this series, we’re still exploring the transition from measurement to understanding. We’ve asked what a model learned. Now we need to know where that learning holds.

The numbers can be misleading

New AI models almost always arrive with performance numbers.

You might see an accuracy of 90%, for example, or a metric called the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).

AUROC measures how well a model distinguishes between people who experience an outcome and those who don’t. A value of 0.5 indicates no better ranking than chance, while 1.0 represents 100% discrimination.

It’s useful information. But before interpreting it, I want to know something about the experiment.

Who was the model tested on?

If the evaluation used patients from the same hospital, the same period, or the same measurement systems as the development data, the model is still relatively close to home.

That can tell us whether it recognizes patterns in familiar conditions. It tells us less about whether those patterns will hold elsewhere.

Researchers address this through external validation: evaluating a model on data independent of its development setting, ideally from other institutions, populations, or time periods.

In other words, we are testing whether the original evidence generalizes to other contexts.

Outside scientific papers, the industry pays little attention to two distinct aspects of model performance.

Consider a kitchen thermometer that consistently reads five degrees too high.

It can still tell you which of two pots is hotter. The ranking is correct.

But if you’re using it to decide whether chicken has reached a safe internal temperature, that five-degree error matters considerably.

A similar distinction applies to clinical prediction models.

Discrimination describes how well a model ranks people by risk. Does it assign higher scores to those more likely to experience the outcome?

Calibration tells us whether the predicted probabilities match observed event rates. For instance, if 100 patients are assigned a 20% risk, do 20 of them experience the event?

A model can have strong discrimination yet suffer from poor calibration.

It may correctly identify which patients are at greater risk while substantially overestimating or underestimating their actual probability of illness.

This distinction matters because clinicians aren’t always interested in ranking patients. They often need to decide whether a patient’s risk is high enough to justify an intervention.

A predicted risk of 20% means something very different if the true risk is closer to 5%, shifting a patient across a clinical treatment threshold.³

When a model moves between hospitals, aspects of its performance can change.

Some models do travel well

Let’s return to the Michigan findings.

Using data from 38,455 hospitalizations between late 2018 and late 2019, researchers evaluated the performance of a widely deployed commercial sepsis prediction model at their own health system.²

About 7% of the patients developed sepsis.

The model achieved an AUROC of 0.63, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.62 to 0.64. That was substantially below the performance reported by its developer.

The practical results are easier to appreciate when translated into a group of 100 hospital stays.

  • Around 7 patients developed sepsis.

  • The model generated alerts for approximately 18 patients.

  • Of the 7 patients who developed sepsis, the model missed about 5.

In other words, the model missed roughly two-thirds of sepsis cases, even though clinicians were inundated with alerts.²

That’s a difficult combination for a clinical tool: substantial workload without reliably identifying the patients it is meant to protect.

The researchers also reported poor calibration. The model’s risk estimates did not adequately match the outcomes observed in their hospital.

The performance reported during development did not materialize at this institution.

However, the narrative does not stop at this disappointing result.

Investigators evaluated an updated version of the commercial model across four major US health systems, analyzing 227,091 hospitalizations. Its AUROC ranged from 0.82 to 0.92, a considerable improvement over the earlier evaluation.⁴

Yet performance still differed across hospitals. Among patients who triggered alerts, only 13% to 26% had sepsis.⁴

Another study evaluated a different sepsis prediction model across 205,005 emergency department visits at four sites. Its AUROC ranged from 0.91 to 0.96, with relatively little variation between sites.⁵

I think these contrasting results are important. The original Michigan findings could easily be used to argue that sepsis prediction models are inherently unreliable. However, the broader evidence seems to contradict this conclusion.

Some models perform well across different settings. Others lose much of their predictive value. And a revised model can behave quite differently from an earlier version.

We cannot decide which will happen from the original performance score alone.

Model transportability is an empirical question, not a safe assumption.

This isn’t just a sepsis problem

The sepsis example illustrates the problem, but the same pattern appears across different areas of medicine.

Sometimes the problem is the institution. Sometimes it’s the population, the passage of time, or even the instrument used to collect the data.

What connects the following examples is a simple question:

What changed between the setting where the evidence was established and the setting where someone wanted to use it?

A different hospital, a different result

Consider a study published in Science examining models developed to predict how people with schizophrenia would respond to antipsychotic treatment.⁶

The researchers evaluated prediction models across independent clinical trials.

Within the trials used to develop them, the models appeared accurate. But when applied to other trials, their predictive performance fell to approximately chance levels.

Combining data from several trials didn’t resolve the problem.⁶

What is striking is that these were not fundamentally different medical conditions or unrelated treatments. Both studies examined the same illness and addressed similar clinical questions, using data gathered under controlled trial conditions.

Even so, patterns that seemed predictive in one study failed to generalize to another.

The setting changed. The predictive relationship did not survive the transition.

This is an uncomfortable but important possibility in science:

A finding can be genuine in the population where it was observed without being broadly applicable.

Different people

Sometimes the biggest difference between the original evidence and its intended application is simply who the research included.

Take medical imaging.

A review of 70 studies involving AI systems for skin disease found that only seven, or 10%, reported participants’ skin tone information.⁸

Without that information, it’s difficult to establish how representative the development and testing populations were. A model can perform beautifully on average, but that doesn't mean it works safely for every demographic subgroup.

We shouldn’t assume this problem began with AI.

Pulse oximeters, the small devices placed on a fingertip to estimate blood oxygen saturation, illustrate how differences in measurement performance can affect clinical care.

In one hospital cohort, among measurements where the pulse oximeter reported oxygen saturation between 92% and 96%, blood tests revealed dangerously low oxygen saturation in 11.7% of measurements from Black patients, compared with 3.6% from White patients.⁹

Across two cohorts, this form of undetected low oxygen was nearly three times as common in Black patients.⁹

Here, the concern begins before any prediction model enters the picture. The measurement itself performs differently across groups.

This matters for AI because models trained on clinical measurements inherit measurement limitations.

A sophisticated algorithm cannot automatically compensate for systematic measurement bias.

The passage of time

Even when a model stays at the same hospital, its performance can change.

Researchers studying acute kidney injury developed prediction models using hospital data from 2003, then evaluated their performance over the following nine years.¹⁰

The models continued to distinguish relatively well between patients at higher and lower risk.

What deteriorated was their calibration.

Over time, the models increasingly overestimated the probability of acute kidney injury, partly because the underlying frequency of the condition had changed.¹⁰

Think back to the thermometer. It still tells you which pot is hotter, but the numbers no longer correspond to the actual temperatures.

This kind of drift is especially easy to overlook when performance monitoring focuses primarily on discrimination.

This is why I find the idea of a permanently validated model difficult to defend. Even when a prediction works today, the evidence supporting it can become less relevant as the world changes.

The instrument itself

This last example is particularly familiar to me.

My research has involved analytical chemistry and lipidomics, where we measure hundreds of molecular signals across large biological sample collections.

In one cohort, we processed approximately 5,000 study samples across 26 analytical batches.

When collecting measurements over so many runs, one learns to be cautious about assuming the instrument behaves identically every day.

Small variations in acquisition parameters, sample handling, or instrument response can introduce differences that don’t come from the differences in the underlying biology.

To correct for those technical variances, we use quality-control samples, internal standards, and correction procedures to distinguish analytical variation from meaningful biological signals.

Without those checks, a change in the instrument could easily be mistaken for a change in the patient.

Now imagine training an AI model using data from one laboratory and deploying it in another.

Both laboratories might report the same biomarker, in the same units, using methods considered equivalent.

But do the measurements behave the same way across the range of values that matters to the model?

That’s an empirical question. I wouldn’t be comfortable answering this question based on the assay name alone.

A model trained on one laboratory’s measurements may depend on subtle characteristics of that measurement system. Moving it to another laboratory can introduce differences the model was never trained to accommodate.

This is simply another version of the same portability problem: assuming that evidence built on one system remains valid when the data comes from another.

That’s why I keep returning to measurement quality in this series.

Before we ask whether an AI model can interpret a biological signal, we need confidence that the signal itself means what we think it means.

(For more on this distinction, see AI Can’t Rescue a Bad Measurement.)

Even good measurements can mislead

The laboratory example leads to a distinction I think deserves its own attention.

Measuring something correctly and predicting what it means are not the same analytical task.

Polygenic risk scores make this particularly clear.

Suppose two people receive genetic testing using the same laboratory technology.

The laboratory identifies which genetic variants each person carries. Assuming the assay performs as intended, those results are biological measurements.

The next step is different.

A statistical model combines the variants and translates them into a risk estimate.

That estimate depends on relationships established in the populations used to develop the model.

Historically, large-scale genetic research behind these scores has focused on people of European ancestry. As a result, a widely cited analysis found that their predictive accuracy was substantially lower in other ancestry groups.⁷

The genetic measurements may be correct in both cases. What differs is how much confidence we can place in the prediction built from those measurements.

There’s no contradiction here. The measurement and the interpretation are two separate claims, each requiring its own supporting evidence.

This is why it matters to distinguish a validated assay from a prediction model evaluated in a particular population.

Knowing that a laboratory measured something correctly doesn’t mean we can interpret its significance equitably for all patients.

The same principle applies to other biomarkers, imaging systems, wearable devices, and clinical AI tools.

The evidence supporting the measurement may be universally strong, while the data supporting its clinical interpretation remain much more limited.

For me, this is one of the most important distinctions in the entire conversation about AI in healthcare.

We often speak about a model’s accuracy as though it were an intrinsic characteristic of the technology. However, a performance score is an evaluation finding, not a guarantee attached to the model wherever it goes.

This is the distinction I have returned to throughout this series: performance as measured versus usefulness as claimed.

The first can be demonstrated experimentally.

The second requires evidence that the experimental findings apply to the decision, population, and setting in question.

There is no such thing as a validated model

That provocative statement is the title of a 2023 paper by researchers who study clinical prediction models.¹¹

Their argument isn’t that validation is unimportant.

It’s that validation is never a permanent property of a model.

Patient populations change. Clinical practices evolve. Measurement procedures differ between institutions and sometimes within the same institution over time.

A model can be successfully evaluated in one setting yet still require further validation before being adopted in another.

I think this is a more useful way of understanding validation.

As a scientist, I’m comfortable with a conditional result. That’s how evidence works. What concerns me is when those conditions disappear from the discussion, leaving behind a performance number that sounds more universal than the research actually supports.

Rather than asking whether a model has been validated, we should ask where, when, on whom, and for what purpose it was evaluated.

For health AI tools, I would look for a few things before putting much confidence in a reported performance score. These are consistent with published guidance for evaluating clinical prediction models.¹²

  • Independent testing. Has the model been evaluated using patients from other hospitals, populations, or time periods, rather than only data closely related to its development?

  • Calibration as well as discrimination. Do its predicted probabilities correspond to observed outcomes, or do they only rank patients reasonably well?

  • Performance across relevant groups. Are the results reported separately for populations in which performance differences could affect care?

  • Monitoring after deployment. Is anyone checking whether the model continues to perform adequately as clinical practice and patient populations change?

These aren’t questions reserved for data scientists.

They’re questions about evidence. About how far that evidence can reasonably be extended.

We expect a medical device to be evaluated under conditions relevant to its intended use. We should expect no less from a model that influences medical decisions.

And if you remember only one question from this article, I’d make it this:

Was it tested anywhere like here?

Here might mean your hospital, your population, your laboratory, or simply the year the model is used.

The Michigan sepsis alarm offers a cautionary example. A system can be developed with good intentions, evaluated using legitimate methods, and still perform poorly when introduced into a different clinical environment.

That doesn’t make AI prediction inherently unreliable.

It means we need to be precise about what the evidence supports.

A performance score tells us what a model achieved under the conditions in which it was evaluated. Claiming the same performance will hold somewhere else requires additional evidence.

That is ultimately the point.

Good evidence matters, but so do the boundaries of what it can tell us.
