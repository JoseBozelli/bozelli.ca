The journey

From molecules
to models

01
Molecules
How can a molecule kill a bacterium?

My scientific career started with a deceptively small question. Studying antimicrobial peptides pulled me into the physical world of biological membranes — how peptides and lipids interact, how molecular structure changes function, and how understanding mechanism can point toward new therapeutic possibilities.

Biochemistry · Membrane biophysics
02
Biological systems
When do lipids become biological actors?

Those questions grew. Lipids stopped being only the membrane a peptide encountered and became biological actors in their own right — molecules involved in signaling, metabolism, and disease. My research moved across rare metabolic disorders, cancer, and neurodegeneration, while the underlying question stayed remarkably consistent: how does molecular biology become function, dysfunction, and potentially, intervention?

Lipid biology · Disease mechanisms
03
Populations
What changes when one sample becomes thousands?

Eventually, the lens pulled back again. At McMaster University, I moved into large-scale epidemiological lipidomics — measuring thousands of samples from a national nutritional study. The challenge was no longer understanding one molecular interaction or disease mechanism. It was making biological measurement reliable enough to reveal patterns across a population.

LC-MS · Lipidomics · Epidemiological data
04
Models
How do we find meaning at that scale?

That change in scale changed the tools I needed. Statistics, data science, machine learning, and AI make it possible to ask different questions of biological data — to find structure across thousands of measurements, test what generalizes, and eventually help turn evidence into decisions. I see those tools as an expansion of the same scientific practice, not a departure from it.

Data science · ML · AI

The scale changed.

How I think
01
Start with the biology.

A model finding a pattern doesn't make that pattern biologically meaningful.

02
Respect the measurement.

Every number has a history: sampling, measurement, processing, and normalization determine what it can legitimately tell us.

03
Use the right tool.

Experiment. Mass spectrometry. Statistics. Machine learning. Sophistication isn't the goal. Understanding is.

Today, the questions keep expanding — across scientific consulting, writing, and data science.

The throughline remains the same: understand the measurement, understand the biology beneath it, and be precise about what the evidence allows us to conclude.
