Scientist · Writer · Advisor
Based in Canada. Working toward problems that matter anywhere.

José Bozelli is a scientist interested in a deceptively simple problem: we can measure more about the human body than ever before, but knowing what those measurements actually mean is much harder.

That question has taken his work from molecules and cell membranes to biomarkers, omics, population-scale data, and increasingly data science, machine learning, and AI.

Different scales. Different tools. Same instinct: understand the biology, respect the measurement, and don't ask the data to say more than it can.

Behind these questions is a career built by moving between scales.

José began in chemistry, studying how molecular structure determines physical behaviour. That curiosity moved into biochemistry and membrane biology: how molecules organize themselves, how proteins interact with their environment, and how seemingly small physical changes can alter biological function.

Over time, the questions grew larger. Molecular mechanisms became disease biology. Controlled experiments expanded into human samples and population-scale omics. And increasingly, the challenge became not simply generating measurements, but deciding which signals could be trusted, how they should be interpreted, and what they actually meant.

That evolution now extends into data science, machine learning and AI: extracting reliable meaning from complex evidence.

Alongside research, José has taught and mentored emerging scientists, developed course material, facilitated advanced scientific discussions and invested in formal training in university teaching. That same instinct — to make difficult ideas understandable without making them simplistic — now shapes his writing and public-facing work.

The questions are getting bigger.

Today, José's work increasingly sits where biology, data and decision-making meet. His interests include biomarkers and omics, evidence quality, health data, scientific AI, and the increasingly difficult question of how people should interpret biological measurements when technology can generate them faster than ever.

Through research, independent projects, consulting development and writing, the aim is the same:

Help turn more data into better understanding — not simply more information.
