About

From molecules to models.
The questions got bigger. So did the toolkit.

Languages — English, Português

My scientific career started with a deceptively small question: how can a molecule kill a bacterium? Studying antimicrobial peptides pulled me into the physical world of biological membranes — how peptides and lipids interact, how molecular structure changes function, and how understanding mechanism can point toward new therapeutic possibilities.

Those questions grew. Lipids stopped being only the membrane a peptide encountered and became biological actors in their own right — molecules involved in signaling, metabolism, and disease. My research moved across rare metabolic disorders, cancer, and neurodegeneration, while the underlying question stayed remarkably consistent: how does molecular biology become function, dysfunction, and potentially, intervention?

Eventually, the lens pulled back again. At McMaster University, I moved into large-scale epidemiological lipidomics — measuring thousands of samples from a national nutritional study. The challenge was no longer understanding one molecular interaction or disease mechanism. It was making biological measurement reliable enough to reveal patterns across a population.

That change in scale changed the tools I needed. Statistics, data science, machine learning, and AI make it possible to ask different questions of biological data — to find structure across thousands of measurements, test what generalizes, and eventually help turn evidence into decisions. I see those tools as an expansion of the same scientific practice, not a departure from it.

How I think

Three habits that
shape every analysis

Start with the biology.

A pattern becomes interesting when there's a plausible biological story underneath it — not simply because a model found it.

Respect the measurement.

Every number has a history: how it was sampled, measured, processed, and normalized determines what it can legitimately tell us.

Use the right tool for the question.

Sometimes that's an experiment. Sometimes mass spectrometry. Sometimes statistics or machine learning. Sophistication isn't the goal; understanding is.
